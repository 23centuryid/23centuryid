Overview and Background

cGMP stands for “current Good Manufacturing Practice.”

It originated from the concept of GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice), a set of standards to ensure product quality and safety in the manufacturing of pharmaceuticals, foods, and other products. The “c” in cGMP indicates current, meaning the present standard based on the latest technologies and knowledge.

This reflects the idea of continuously incorporating advances in manufacturing technology and quality management methods over time, and always managing production to the latest level.

cGMP is an evolution of GMP systems established across countries since the 1960s, with the overarching purpose of preventing health harm caused by pharmaceuticals and foods.

For example, in the United States, GMP became mandatory with the 1962 amendments to drug law, and today detailed rules are set out as cGMP in Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR) under the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

cGMP is an internationally shared concept and is positioned as a fundamental element of quality assurance across a wide range of product fields, including pharmaceuticals, foods, cosmetics, and medical devices.

Main Requirements and Standards

Under cGMP, manufacturers must comply with specific, concrete requirements.
These requirements are foundational principles that ensure quality and safety across the entire manufacturing process. Major points common to many national guidelines include:

Maintaining a Hygienic Manufacturing Environment

Manufacturing facilities must be kept clean and sanitary at all times. Environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, cleanliness, etc.) are managed appropriately to prevent foreign-matter contamination and cross-contamination (contamination due to product intermixing).

Process Definition, Control, and Validation

Manufacturing processes are clearly defined in SOPs (standard operating procedures), and critical steps are appropriately controlled and monitored. Steps that affect quality are subject to prior validation to verify that the process consistently meets specified quality requirements.

When changes occur, their impact on quality is evaluated and, as needed, re-validation is performed.

Documentation and Recordkeeping

Procedures and standards are documented in clear language and observed on the shop floor. Actions taken during each step are retained as manufacturing records so that activities can be traced for each lot.

Deviations and anomalies are recorded in detail, with root-cause investigation and corrective actions implemented as necessary. This ensures traceability so that, if problems arise later, the step where the defect occurred can be identified.

Employee Training and Hygiene Management

Personnel engaged in manufacturing and quality control must receive adequate education and training.
Personnel involved in production must possess appropriate knowledge and skills and execute procedures correctly; in addition, personal hygiene (e.g., handwashing, wearing work garments) is managed to prevent contamination and errors due to human factors.

Quality Control and Change Control

Appropriate quality testing (e.g., on intermediates and finished products) is performed, and only items conforming to specifications are released. When changes occur in processes or raw materials, their impact on product quality is evaluated, and—where necessary—authorities are notified and/or re-evaluation is performed.

Distribution and Corrective Actions

Measures are taken during distribution after release to prevent quality deterioration (e.g., maintaining appropriate packaging and storage conditions). A recall system is established so that, if a problem is identified in the market, affected lots can be retrieved promptly.

Complaints and defect reports from the market are investigated, causes are determined, and preventive measures are implemented.

These are examples; cGMP also defines many other requirements, including calibration and maintenance of equipment (periodic calibration of instruments and cleaning/inspection of equipment), incoming control of raw materials (supplier qualification and raw material testing), and independence of the quality assurance function (a QA authority independent from manufacturing making final decisions).

Most requirements are based on the concepts of a Quality Management System (QMS). Organizations are expected not only to “make good products,” but to build a system that “consistently makes good products.”

Each company is responsible for establishing and operating the most efficient concrete measures to meet cGMP requirements in a manner appropriate to its business.

Significance and Benefits of Compliance/Certification

Ensuring compliance with cGMP is of critical legal and business importance for manufacturers.

From a regulatory perspective, GMP compliance is a prerequisite when manufacturers and marketers of products such as pharmaceuticals and foods seek authorization from national authorities.

For example, the U.S. FDA stipulates that products manufactured by methods or in facilities not conforming to cGMP are deemed “adulterated.”

This means that even if a product itself passes testing, it may not be sold if the manufacturing environment or procedures violate cGMP—demonstrating that cGMP adherence is a legal obligation.

Violations of pharmaceutical GMP ministerial ordinances can lead to administrative action or production suspension. Thus, cGMP compliance is a prerequisite for business continuity and indispensable for meeting regulatory requirements.

From a quality assurance standpoint, following cGMP enables assurance of product safety and efficacy and maintenance of consistent quality.

Products manufactured under proper control exhibit less variability and more stable lot-to-lot quality. This builds customer trust and reduces the risk of complaints and recalls.

By reducing process waste and errors, manufacturing efficiency and yield can improve—delivering economic benefits. In other words, cGMP is not merely a “costly regulation,” but a long-term investment that enhances corporate credibility and productivity.

Furthermore, cGMP compliance is a valuable credential in international business. Many countries require GMP compliance certificates for imports, and in dealings with overseas partners, stating that “our plant conforms to cGMP” demonstrates quality assurance capability.

This facilitates smoother entry into new markets and overseas expansion.

Especially in pharmaceuticals and medical devices, inspection outcomes by national authorities may be mutually recognized (e.g., among PIC/S members), so a high rating may be accepted across multiple countries.

Overall, adherence to cGMP achieves both regulatory compliance and product quality assurance, strengthening a company’s social credibility.

Impact on Products, Services, and Consumers

Products shipped from plants that implement cGMP appropriately are safer and more trustworthy for consumers.

For medicines, the amount of active ingredient is consistent from lot to lot, and contamination by impurities that could cause side effects is minimized. For foods and supplements, strict measures prevent foreign matter and harmful microbes, assuring products that are safe to consume.

Thus, by choosing products from companies that adhere to cGMP, consumers can significantly reduce health risks due to quality defects.

From a service perspective, when pharmaceutical companies manufacture and manage quality under cGMP, the quality of medicines supplied steadily to medical institutions and pharmacies is assured.

Healthcare professionals can provide such medicines to patients with confidence, and patients can receive pharmacotherapy managed appropriately. The same applies in the food industry: products from cGMP-compliant processing facilities are safer, building trust among distributors, retailers, and consumers.

This also contributes to brand value, establishing the reputation that “products from this manufacturer are reliable.”

Conversely, the impact of non-compliance with cGMP can be severe.

If products from companies with poor quality control reach the market, defects and health harm may directly affect consumers. In the event of serious defects, large-scale recalls and lawsuits may occur, undermining consumer trust in the company.

Therefore, from the perspective of consumer protection, cGMP compliance is a social responsibility and an essential element for a company’s sustained development.

While the term cGMP may be unfamiliar to many consumers, its benefits are delivered daily as the safety of medicines and foods they use.

Relevant International Bodies and Regulators

Key regulators related to cGMP include the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which strictly oversees cGMP compliance for pharmaceutical and food manufacturers; details are set out in Title 21 of the CFR.

For pharmaceuticals, cGMP regulations appear in 21 CFR Parts 210/211, and FDA inspectors periodically audit manufacturing sites.

In Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and national authorities conduct inspections under the EU-GMP guidelines.

Internationally, PIC/S (Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme) promotes mutual recognition of GMP inspection results and harmonization of standards among national authorities.

The WHO (World Health Organization) has developed WHO-GMP guidelines—often referenced by countries when building GMP systems.

At the private level, ISO provides international standards for quality management (e.g., ISO 9001), which share principles with GMP such as continual improvement and risk management.

Thanks to these international bodies and frameworks, GMP concepts are widely shared globally today, raising manufacturing quality for pharmaceuticals and foods worldwide.
