Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD) is a condition in which arteries carrying blood from the heart to the legs narrow or become blocked due to atherosclerosis (plaque buildup), leading to restricted blood flow.

Due to inadequate blood supply to the lower extremities, patients present with symptoms such as calf pain, exertional paresthesia resolving with rest, and in severe cases, intense leg pain even at rest, poorly healing wounds, or digital ulcers.

Advanced Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD) progresses to Critical Limb Ischemia (CLI), a serious condition that, if left untreated, can result in gangrene and, in the worst-case scenario, limb amputation.

When arteries narrow due to plaque, blood flow deteriorates, and the muscles and skin in the legs do not receive sufficient oxygen and nutrients, which causes the symptoms of PAD.

For PAD, treatment primarily aims to prevent progression and alleviate symptoms through lifestyle modifications and pharmacological therapy (e.g., medications to thin the blood or dilate blood vessels, etc.).

Additionally, when vascular occlusion is severe, catheter-based angioplasty procedures (e.g., percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) or stent placement) or surgical vascular reconstruction, such as bypass, are performed.

Although many patients improve with this treatment, there are numerous cases where atherosclerosis is extensive throughout the lower extremities, making surgical intervention difficult, or patients are unable to tolerate surgery due to severe complications.

Indeed, approximately 10-30% of patients with severe Critical Limb Ischemia (CLI) are considered "no-option" for vascular reconstruction, and effective treatment options are currently limited. In these challenging cases, pain and ulceration often do not resolve, and ultimately, partial limb amputation may be required.

Amidst the limitations of current medical treatments, there is a pressing need for the development of novel therapeutic approaches for patients desperately hoping to preserve their own limbs.

Peripheral Artery Disease and Stem Cell Therapy: New Prospects in Regenerative Medicine

Therefore, recent attention has been directed towards a novel approach in regenerative medicine, specifically utilizing stem cell therapy. Stem cell therapy is a medical treatment method that employs "stem cells"—the foundational cells of our body—to promote the regeneration of damaged tissues.

For limbs experiencing compromised blood flow due to arterial occlusion, the objective is to administer stem cells to induce endogenous neovascularization, thereby enhancing perfusion. Rather than surgically constructing artificial bypass pathways, this approach promotes the formation of natural bypasses (collateral circulation) by leveraging the inherent regenerative capabilities of stem cells.

Indeed, for CLI patients who lack conventional treatment options, this type of regenerative medicine approach is anticipated as a novel strategy.

There are various types of stem cells, but those primarily used in regenerative medicine for peripheral artery disease are mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) or mononuclear cells derived from bone marrow and peripheral blood.

Specifically, MSCs can be extracted from various body parts such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, and the umbilical cord. By culturing and expanding these cells, the required number of cells can be secured, making MSCs a potent cell source for regenerative medicine.

MSCs possess superior capabilities in producing growth factors that regenerate blood vessels and suppress inflammation, and have been shown to contribute to the improvement of peripheral artery disease. Of recent interest are umbilical cord-derived stem cells isolated from Wharton's Jelly, the gelatinous tissue within the umbilical cord (Wharton's Jelly-derived MSC, WJ-MSC).

These are stem cells derived from placental tissue collected at birth, offering numerous advantages, including high proliferative and differentiation capacity. They eliminate the need to extract cells from the patient's own body, thereby reducing the burden, and can be made available to a large number of patients by culturing and expanding them.

Additionally, it has been reported that WJ-MSCs possess a high capacity to secrete substances involved in angiogenesis and anti-inflammation, and hold great promise as a cell source for regenerative medicine in peripheral artery disease.

Mechanism of Action of Stem Cell Therapy for Peripheral Artery Disease

Stem cell therapy is considered effective for peripheral artery disease because stem cells are "multifunctional therapists" that simultaneously perform multiple roles. The following mechanisms of action are broadly known for stem cell therapy.

Promoting the growth of new blood vessels (angiogenesis promotion):

Stem cells secrete a large number of substances, including growth factors and cytokines, which act as angiogenic signals in damaged tissue. This activates surrounding cells and promotes the elongation of new capillaries, leading to the formation of a natural vascular bypass at the cellular level (a process known as collateral circulation formation).

For example, bone marrow-derived mononuclear cells contain endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) and, when incorporated into the walls of newly formed blood vessels as part of the nascent vasculature, are also reported to strengthen vascular tissue.

Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory actions:

In regions of compromised blood flow, beyond the tissue damage resulting from vascular occlusion, an inflammatory response develops. This inflammation then further damages blood vessels and tissues, creating a vicious cycle.

Stem cells also secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines that mitigate inflammation, thereby exerting a protective effect on a hyperactive immune system. This action leads to the alleviation of unnecessary inflammatory tissue damage and pain, fostering a conducive environment for healing.

Tissue protection and repair promotion (cellular protection and anti-fibrotic effects):

Stem cell-secreted factors not only possess angiogenic and anti-inflammatory properties but also protect tissue cells and inhibit fibrosis.

By delivering cytoprotective factors that promote cellular survival and resilience to hypoxic skeletal muscle cells, tissue necrosis can be mitigated. Furthermore, severe inflammation during periods of oxygen deprivation often leads to the stiffening and hardening (fibrosis) of injured tissue. Stem cells possess the capacity to prevent this process.

Stem cell therapy involves the injection of cells such as bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs), bone marrow mononuclear cells (BM-MNCs), or endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) from peripheral blood into ischemic leg regions. Initially, these cells secrete bioactive substances like growth factors, cytokines, and enzymes to promote angiogenesis. Consequently, this facilitates the formation of new capillaries in the surrounding tissues, leading to improved blood flow.

Furthermore, stem cells secrete anti-inflammatory substances, suppressing damage caused by hyperactive immune responses. In addition, they also serve to prevent fibrosis, a condition characterized by the stiffening of damaged tissue.

These stem cell interventions protect cells, such as skeletal muscle, that have been compromised by oxygen and nutrient deprivation, thereby facilitating the preservation of muscle and skin structure and function.

Thus, stem cell therapy not only induces angiogenesis but also facilitates blood flow restoration, modulates inflammation, and promotes tissue protection and repair through various mechanisms, thereby contributing to the alleviation of symptoms in peripheral artery disease and the promotion of recovery.

Evidence of Effectiveness of Stem Cell Therapy for Peripheral Artery Disease

Stem cell therapy holds theoretical promise, but the critical question is its actual effectiveness.

Notably, over the last two decades, extensive clinical research on stem cell therapy in patients with Peripheral Artery Disease (PAD) and Critical Limb Ischemia (CLI) has been conducted globally. Consequently, favorable data demonstrating the efficacy of stem cell therapy has progressively accumulated.

For instance, in Japan, a 2002 trial involved the injection of bone marrow cells into the legs of CLI patients, reporting alleviation of leg pain, accelerated ulcer healing, and even a reduction in amputation rates. This study (the TACT trial by Tanabe and Yuasa) garnered international attention and became a precursor to cell therapy for PAD.

Recently, not only small trials but also meta-analyses integrating results from numerous clinical trials have been published. One such study integrated an analysis of 23 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving patients with CLI (n=962), and the efficacy and safety of stem cell therapy were evaluated (PubMed ID: 29977308).

Based on the analysis, the stem cell group demonstrated the following improvements compared to the group receiving only conventional treatment:

  • Recalcitrant leg ulcers are demonstrating a trend of improved healing.
  • Angiogenesis indicators were significantly elevated (new capillary formation was promoted).
  • Increased blood flow and oxygen indicators such as the ankle-brachial index (ABI) and transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcO₂).
  • Reduction of foot pain at rest and increased pain-free walking distance (improved ambulation)
  • Reduction in the risk of foot amputation (lower limb amputation) within a specific period.

In this way, patients receiving stem cell therapy have demonstrated improvements in objective indicators of blood flow, symptoms, and motor function. Furthermore, a reduction in the proportion progressing to major amputation has been shown.

While some research indicates variability in efficacy rates, it is generally suggested that limbs considered unsalvageable by conventional treatment could potentially be salvaged with stem cell therapy.

Furthermore, clinical research utilizing novel stem cell sources, specifically umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs), is emerging. For instance, a Phase I clinical trial reported in 2024 involved the injection of allogeneic WJ-MSCs into the leg muscles of patients with severe critical limb ischemia (CLI) complicated by diabetes, with subsequent observation of their safety and efficacy.

Consequently, all patients who received the treatment were free from leg (including toe) amputation for six months, and no serious adverse events were reported. Furthermore, six months post-treatment, significant improvements were observed in leg pain (VAS score), the duration of pain-free walking, and leg function scores (FADI).

Despite the small patient cohort, this is a significant report demonstrating substantial improvement achieved with the latest WJ-MSC therapy, characterized by "reduced leg pain and improved ambulation" (PubMed ID: 38112806). Furthermore, various clinical trials for PAD therapy utilizing diverse stem cells are ongoing worldwide, and new evidence continues to accumulate.

This body of research moves stem cell therapy closer to becoming a more established treatment option.

Safety of Stem Cell Therapy for Peripheral Artery Disease and Side Effects

Regarding new treatment methods, safety and adverse effects may also be a concern.

In summary, stem cell therapy for peripheral artery disease has demonstrated overall good safety in current studies. An integrated analysis of numerous clinical trials identified no serious side effects (adverse events) associated with stem cell therapy.

Notably, there have been no reports of severe immune rejection reactions or treatment-related deaths, and it is deemed that no significant safety concerns exist. Conversely, for CLI patients for whom conventional treatment is not an option, the potential benefits may outweigh the risks.

Indeed, the reported side effects were notably mild. These included transient pain or swelling at the cell injection site, and occasional mild fever or fatigue observed during infusion. All such effects were transient and resolved with appropriate management.

Cases of minor internal bleeding (hematoma) resulting from injections have also been observed; however, these resolved spontaneously and were not clinically significant. Therefore, no complications more severe than these have been reported.

Additionally, regarding the use of allogeneic (donor-derived) stem cells, anticipated rejection reactions have not been observed. This is attributed to the inherent immunomodulatory functions of MSCs, which make it challenging for the host immune system to attack or reject the introduced cells.

From a safety perspective, stem cell therapy is a minimally invasive treatment administered via injection or infusion. This approach significantly reduces the physical burden on patients compared to major surgical interventions, such as extensive bypass procedures in thoracic or peripheral blood vessels.

Furthermore, even in the previously mentioned clinical trials of umbilical cord-derived stem cells, no adverse effects were reported during the six-month follow-up period. Beyond the inherent safety of the stem cell therapy itself, the fact that the treatment process is a patient-friendly method constitutes a significant advantage.

Based on the above, stem cell therapy for peripheral artery disease is considered a novel and relatively safe treatment option.

Conclusion

For patients with peripheral artery disease (PAD), stem cell therapy is a regenerative medicine that holds the potential to bring new hope even to severe cases where light is difficult to see.

While not yet widely established as a mainstream treatment method, it is an emerging, cutting-edge field where active clinical research is being conducted both domestically and internationally, and is being introduced into the medical field. It is also anticipated that, when used in conjunction with conventional treatments, synergistic effects will be achieved, and treatment outcomes for vascular diseases can be further enhanced in the future.

The important thing is not to give up on "there is no more treatment."

With advancements in stem cell therapy, a limb that previously required amputation could potentially be salvaged. This also offers significant reassurance for patients and their families.

For individuals experiencing peripheral artery disease or those interested in stem cell therapy, it is recommended to consult a specialized medical institution or their treating physician. They should expect to receive a comprehensive explanation of the most appropriate options, informed by the latest research trends.
