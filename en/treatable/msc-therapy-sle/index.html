Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease in which the body's immune system mistakenly attacks its own tissues, causing inflammation in various organs and tissues.

The disease course is characterized by periods of remission (when the disease subsides) and exacerbation (when it worsens). Current primary treatment involves the administration of steroids and immunosuppressants. Unfortunately, in over half of patients, exacerbations cannot be entirely prevented.

Furthermore, as the medication dosage increases to suppress disease symptoms, the risk of serious adverse effects, such as severe infections, also increases. Long-term steroid use leads to adverse effects (e.g., osteoporosis, diabetes, severe infections) which significantly impair patients' quality of life.

Furthermore, only one of the newly emerging biological drugs for SLE (belimumab) has been approved by the US FDA in recent decades, and even this drug does not provide adequate efficacy for all patients.

Consequently, current treatments for SLE are limited in terms of both efficacy and adverse effects, and patients are seeking novel therapeutic methods that are safer and more fundamental.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus and Stem Cell Therapy: Novel Prospects for Regenerative Medicine

While conventional drug therapy focuses on "suppressing" symptoms, stem cell therapy through regenerative medicine is a novel approach that aims to "repair and normalize" the body's intrinsic functions.

In autoimmune diseases such as SLE, the immune system, which is intended to defend the body, becomes dysfunctional and attacks self-tissues. Stem cell therapy holds potential to "reset" these abnormal immune reactions and guide the body back to a state of normalcy.

In particular, a type of stem cell known as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) has garnered significant attention, with research and clinical trials currently underway worldwide. MSCs can be harvested from sources such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord, with options for using the patient's own cells (autologous) or cells from a donor (allogeneic).

Among these, Wharton's Jelly-derived stem cells (WJ-MSC), contained within the umbilical cord, are considered highly promising. Wharton's Jelly is a gelatinous tissue within the umbilical cord, obtained at birth.

MSCs derived from this site are reported to exhibit lower immunogenicity compared to bone marrow-derived MSCs and possess high proliferative capacity. Therefore, it is anticipated that a large quantity of stem cells can be provided with minimal burden, and they are expected to be suitable for administration to patients.

Stem cell therapy stands at the forefront of regenerative medicine, offering new hope even for challenging cases of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) – specifically, those refractory to conventional treatments – which previously had no effective therapeutic options.

Mechanism of Action of Stem Cell Therapy for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

MSCs are considered effective for SLE because they can address the disease's pathophysiology from multiple angles. We will explain three main mechanisms by which stem cell therapy works in SLE.

Immune Recalibration

MSCs modulate overactive immune systems and restore balance. Imagine MSCs engaging with the immune system's 'command center,' effectively re-educating it to recognize that self should not be the target of attack.

Specifically, MSC upregulates regulatory T cells that suppress exaggerated self-reactivity and inhibits the aberrant activity of autoantibody-producing B cells. In this manner, MSC effectively curbs immune overactivity.

Anti-inflammatory action and tissue repair.

MSCs secrete numerous active substances and small secretory vesicles, collectively referred to as the "secretome." These substances act systemically to mitigate inflammation and promote the repair of damaged tissues.

For instance, by mitigating pro-inflammatory substances while simultaneously providing growth factors that promote tissue repair and regeneration, it is anticipated that the healing potential of damaged organs can be improved.

Organ protection and function:

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) frequently causes damage to vital organs such as the kidneys and lungs. MSCs alleviate attacks on these organs and function to halt damage. Indeed, in animal models and preliminary studies, MSC administration has improved indicators of renal function and histologically reduced organ tissue damage.

Stem cell therapy is a comprehensive "curative" and "modulatory" approach that addresses the pathophysiology of SLE from multiple directions. In essence, it is a treatment method that curbs the dysregulated immune system while aiding in the repair of damaged tissues. While research is ongoing to elucidate more detailed mechanisms, patients anticipate this as a "treatment that enhances the body's constitution and approaches the root cause of the disease."

Evidence of Effectiveness of Stem Cell Therapy for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

What is the true efficacy of stem cell therapy? To date, small-scale clinical trials and patient case series reports have been conducted in various countries worldwide, with the majority reporting promising outcomes. The following presents some evidence.

Clinical Trial of Umbilical MSC Transplantation in 15 Refractory SLE Patients (China)

A study investigated the intravenous administration of allogeneic Wharton's Jelly Mesenchymal Stem Cells (WJ-MSCs) to 15 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) patients with high disease activity and an inadequate response to conventional treatment. Approximately one year post-administration, results indicated a dramatic increase in disease activity across all patients, coupled with a decline in organ damage indicators, including renal function.

Notably, no serious adverse events were reported. Although some patients experienced a recurrence of renal damage (proteinuria) one year following treatment initiation, the majority maintained a state of remission.

Long-term Follow-up Results of MSC Therapy (6 Years)

A 6-year follow-up report on a subset of patients (overlapping with the preceding report) demonstrated that all patients tolerated the treatment well, and in the long term, there was no increased risk of developing cancer or serious infections.

It was reported that disease activity indicators (SLEDAI scores) showed a sustained decrease following treatment, and the reduction in autoantibodies and improvement in renal function were maintained. This finding, demonstrating the long-term maintenance of both safety and effectiveness, provides crucial evidence that enhances confidence in stem cell therapy.

Phase I Clinical Trial in the United States (Supported by LFA)

WJ-MSC Phase I clinical trials have also yielded promising results, with support from the Lupus Foundation of America (LFA) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

In this trial, disease activity significantly decreased in 5 out of 6 patients with refractory SLE following MSC administration, and this effect persisted for one year. As a secondary benefit, successful steroid dose reduction was also achieved, with 5 out of 6 patients reducing their steroid dosage to 10 mg/day or less.

In terms of safety, no significant issues were identified, with only mild nausea and sensations of warmth and perspiration observed in a few patients. Based on these results, a Phase II trial targeting a larger patient population is underway.

Beyond this research, reports on the use of MSCs continue to emerge globally, and experts have evaluated that "MSC therapy is a promising treatment method that can be effective against SLE unresponsive to conventional treatment."

It is important to note that it remains in the clinical trial phase and is not yet considered an established standard treatment. Nevertheless, the currently available evidence strongly indicates the efficacy of stem cell therapy.

Specifically, beneficial findings directly related to patients' long-term prognosis, such as improvement in renal lesions and steroid-sparing effects, should not be overlooked.

Safety of Stem Cell Therapy for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus and Side Effects

When considering a new treatment, "safety" is a primary concern for patients and their families.

Although stem cell therapy is not yet recognized as a covered treatment, cumulative clinical research indicates it has a relatively high safety profile.

As previously described, clinical trials demonstrated no serious side effects and virtually no complications. In trials conducted in the United States, only mild nausea and hot flashes were reported. Furthermore, long-term studies in China indicated no increased risk of cancer development or serious infections.

Autologous stem cells inherently eliminate concerns regarding rejection. Even when derived from allogeneic donors, serious rejection issues are not a significant concern. Furthermore, the use of stem cells obtained from birth byproducts, such as WJ-MSC, is non-invasive for the donor, and ethical barriers are minimal.

In light of these findings, ongoing clinical research has reported no significant safety concerns regarding stem cell therapy, suggesting that patients can approach it with confidence.

This high safety profile is one reason for optimism about stem cell therapy.

Conclusion

Stem cell therapy for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus is an area within regenerative medicine research that is currently receiving particular attention.

When challenging cases, refractory to conventional treatment, are considered in light of an approach that targets the body's inherent constitution for fundamental improvement, this strategy is substantiated by extensive clinical trials and research.

Indeed, extensive research and time are still required before it can be established as a standard treatment. However, the gradually accumulating body of knowledge, both domestically and internationally, demonstrates tangible progress.

For patients and families affected by SLE, the emergence of stem cell therapy as a novel treatment option offers significant hope. In the future of medicine, these intrinsic "repair agents" of the body – stem cells – may be capable of repairing disease-damaged tissues and guiding the dysregulated immune system back to normal function, thereby making such treatments a routine part of medical care.

We are advancing incrementally towards that day, and the opportunity for stem cell therapy will undoubtedly materialize.

Medical professionals and researchers are working collaboratively and diligently towards a future where patients can confidently receive these benefits. We encourage you to await future developments with an optimistic outlook.
