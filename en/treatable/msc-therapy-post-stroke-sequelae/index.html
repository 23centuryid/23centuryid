For individuals who have experienced a stroke and their families, primary consequences such as partial paralysis and gait disturbances pose a significant burden on daily living.

However, in recent years, a new "regenerative medicine" approach, known as Stem Cell Therapy, has garnered attention as a beacon of hope for post-stroke patients such as these.

Specifically, treatment utilizing Wharton's Jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) obtained from the placenta and umbilical cord has demonstrated promising recovery outcomes.

Post-Stroke Management and Current Treatment Challenges

When an individual experiences a stroke (cerebral infarction or cerebral hemorrhage), common sequelae often include unilateral paralysis resulting in impaired mobility, or difficulty articulating words fluently.

In modern medicine, acute management immediately following a stroke includes fibrinolytic therapy (medications to dissolve blood clots) and catheter-based endovascular interventions, which have rapidly advanced and saved many lives.

However, no therapeutic method has yet been proven to radically repair brain neurons and tissue damaged by stroke.

Consequently, advanced symptoms such as paralysis often persist for extended periods, and even with rehabilitation, recovery frequently plateaus over time.

In fact, many patients continue to experience paralysis or language impairments for extended periods, necessitating long-term care and posing a significant burden on both patients and their families.

The inability to adequately ameliorate this post-stroke disability has become a significant challenge, and expectations for novel treatment modalities are consequently increasing.

Stem Cell Therapy for Post-Stroke Care and New Possibilities in Regenerative Medicine

Stem Cell Therapy, a branch of regenerative medicine, has garnered attention in efforts to address post-stroke sequelae.

Regenerative medicine, in simpler terms, is a type of treatment that aims to regenerate damaged tissues and organs within the body.

Stem Cell Therapy is a method characterized by the introduction of cells from the patient themselves (autologous cells) or cells provided by another donor (allogeneic cells) into the body to restore lost tissue function.

Stem cells are capable of differentiating into various tissues, and cells from diverse sources have been investigated, ranging from bone marrow and adipose tissue to birth-derived placenta and umbilical cord.

Among these, mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSC), extracted from the jelly-like tissue in the umbilical cord known as "Wharton's Jelly," have received particular attention in recent years.

Wharton's Jelly-derived stem cells are umbilical cord-derived cells obtained at birth and readily collected. They are characterized by a low propensity for immune rejection, even when allogeneically transplanted.

These stem cells possess a protective barrier that shields them from the maternal immune system during fetal development. This characteristic enables them to resist immune attack (rejection) even when transplanted into another individual.

By utilizing WJ-MSCs with such superior properties, treatment can be administered without the difficulty or burden of autologous cell harvesting.

Global research is currently underway into adjunctive post-stroke therapies utilizing stem cells, offering the prospect that patients may achieve neurological functional recovery previously unattainable through conventional treatments.

For example, stem cells are administered into the body via intravenous (IV) injection or by inserting a needle through the hip bone into the cerebrospinal fluid space (intrathecal administration) to repair damaged brain tissue.

Mechanism of Action of Stem Cell Therapy for Post-Stroke Recovery

Imagine the brain as a garden.

In the "garden" compromised by the impact of a stroke, harmful inflammation (akin to weeds) proliferates, and vital cells (nerve cells) are unable to thrive.

Then, the incoming stem cells act as farmers, removing 'weeds' (improving the tissue microenvironment), spreading 'fertilizer' (growth factors), and helping 'new leaves' (new nerve cells and blood vessels) to grow.

The main expected effects of Stem Cell Therapy are as follows.

Suppressing inflammation and preparing a healing environment:

Stem cells congregate at the site of injury, producing substances that modulate excessive inflammatory responses and restore immune homeostasis. This functions to quell the 'inflammatory fire' within the damaged brain and establish a milieu conducive to healing.

This mitigates secondary neuronal damage and establishes a foundation for recovery.

Promoting the regeneration of blood vessels and new nerve cells.

Growth factors and cytokines secreted by potent stem cells strongly promote angiogenesis (new capillary formation) and neural regeneration.

For example, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) released from stem cells have been shown to protect damaged nerve cells and promote the growth of new nerve sprouts.

This restores blood and nutrient supply to the damaged brain, promoting neuronal survival and growth.

Facilitating the reorganization of brain neural networks.

It is expected that Stem Cell Therapy promotes the remodeling and reconnection of disrupted neural circuits in the brain.

In animal experiments, it has been reported that stem cells enhance the formation of new synaptic connections between neurons. This mechanism is akin to reconstructing signal transmission pathways, potentially by establishing alternative routes.

As a result, it became possible for the remaining brain regions to assume new roles and compensate for functions, for example, by controlling paralyzed limbs through alternative pathways.

Completing the missing cell:

Despite their limited numbers, there is evidence to suggest that injected stem cells may differentiate into neural cells themselves or vascular cells, thereby replacing damaged ones. In other words, they function to directly replenish the damaged cellular components.

However, current understanding suggests that, rather than the stem cells themselves persisting long-term in the brain and continuing to function, the primary effects are attributed to substances released by these stem cells (known as secretome).

In animal studies, it has been reported that even after transplanted stem cells are cleared from the body over time, substantial therapeutic effects persist, attributed to factors secreted during that period.

Evidence of the Effectiveness of Stem Cell Therapy for a Post-Stroke Cohort

The effectiveness of Stem Cell Therapy in ameliorating post-stroke sequelae has become increasingly evident through various recent clinical studies. A majority of early-phase clinical trials (Phase I/II) conducted worldwide have reported promising results.

Herein, we will present several pieces of scientific evidence.

Illustrative Improvement through Repeated Interventions in Chronic Stroke Patients:

A clinical study was conducted in which patients in the chronic phase, several months to years post-stroke onset, received multiple treatments with Wharton's Jelly-derived stem cells.

In this study, upon periodic injection of stem cells into the intrathecal space (the area surrounding the spinal cord, typically accessed via the lumbar region), improvements in language disorders were observed in some cases (manifesting as clearer speech). Additionally, cases reporting enhanced motor function in paralyzed limbs were also documented.

Notably, all patients receiving treatment demonstrated an enhanced level of independence in their daily lives compared to their previous state. Additionally, some patients exhibited improved communication skills.

Conversely, in the control group, which only received conventional standard treatment, only a small proportion of patients demonstrated significant improvement during the same period. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that no serious side effects were reported in this Stem Cell Therapy.

At the one-year post-treatment follow-up, safety and efficacy were maintained.

Report on functional recovery through intravenous administration.

In separate clinical trials where stem cells were administered via intravenous infusion to post-stroke patients and their progress monitored, the stem cell group demonstrated significant improvement in neurological deficit scores (NIH Stroke Scale) and independence in activities of daily living (Barthel Index and Modified Rankin Scale).

The Stem Cell Therapy group demonstrated significant functional improvement compared to the control (placebo) group, with no serious adverse events reported. The study was conducted as a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, thus ensuring the high scientific reliability of its findings.

Research and other reviews:

Beyond the aforementioned, numerous clinical studies of varying scales, conducted across diverse countries worldwide, have consistently reported improvements in motor function and neurological symptoms attributable to Stem Cell Therapy.

For example, even in trials conducted by Japanese and Korean groups using autologous bone marrow-derived stem cells, improvements in paralysis have been indicated. Furthermore, in acute stroke trials conducted in the West, patients who received stem cells have also been reported to demonstrate a better functional prognosis.

Based on this accumulating evidence, Stem Cell Therapy is realizing the hope that residual paralysis, even after sustained rehabilitation, may improve.

Specifically, Wharton's Jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) possess a wide array of secreted substances (cytokines, growth factors, etc.) and potent immunomodulatory capabilities, making them highly promising for repairing post-stroke damage.

At the experimental level, it has been found that WJ-MSCs exhibit a potent neuroprotective function (neuroprotection), and in clinical studies, similar or even more promising results have been obtained compared to stem cells from other sources.

As clinical trials involving a greater number of subjects continue, the effectiveness of WJ-MSC will be further substantiated.

Safety of Stem Cell Therapy in Post-Stroke Patients and Adverse Effects

In the context of novel therapies, safety is a paramount consideration.

Fortunately, across numerous clinical trials of Stem Cell Therapy conducted to date, very few serious adverse events have been reported.

Specifically, Wharton's Jelly-derived stem cells, as previously mentioned, exhibit low immunogenicity. This characteristic means that even when using allogeneic cells, serious rejection reactions or immunological complications are not readily triggered.

In clinical research, serious inflammatory or allergic reactions attributable to stem cell administration have not been observed.

While it cannot be definitively stated that there are absolutely no adverse effects, the majority of observed side effects are transient and mild. For example, among patients who received stem cell infusions, a few instances of transient fever or headache have been reported. However, these symptoms are temporary and typically resolve without issue with appropriate management.

When stem cells are administered via the lumbar spine, transient headaches may occur, similar to those experienced after a lumbar puncture. However, these symptoms typically resolve within a few days.

It is important to note that stem cells themselves possess characteristics that make them inherently resistant to malignant transformation (cancer), and there have been no reports of transplanted stem cells in previous clinical applications leading to the formation of new tumors.

It is believed that stem cells do not continuously proliferate excessively within the body; rather, after a specific period, they fulfill their designated functions and subsequently diminish.

Furthermore, even when using stem cells extracted from umbilical cord blood or allogeneic umbilical cord tissue, donor screening (e.g., infection tests) and strict quality management during cell culture are performed.

Cells isolated and expanded from provided umbilical cords undergo safety testing in accordance with Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP). Only cells that pass these tests are utilized for therapy. Through this process, the risk of infection and foreign body contamination is reduced to an extremely low level.

Overall, stem cell therapy is considered a treatment method with a high safety profile.

No serious, life-threatening risks have been reported at this time, and it is assessed that there is minimal significant concern for patients.
