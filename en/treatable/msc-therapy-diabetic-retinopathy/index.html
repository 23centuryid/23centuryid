Diabetic Retinopathy and Current Treatment Challenges

Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) is a condition in which the small blood vessels of the retina are damaged by diabetes, leading to vision loss. When blood glucose levels remain elevated over a prolonged period, the retinal blood vessels become blocked or leak, causing oxygen deprivation in the retina and an inability to maintain normal visual function.

As the disease progresses, abnormal new blood vessels (neovascularization) grow on the retina, causing hemorrhage and retinal detachment, which can lead to blindness. Currently, treatment for diabetic retinopathy primarily includes the following symptomatic therapies:

  • Kontrol gula darah dan pemantauan: Hal pertama adalah mengelola kadar gula darah dengan baik. Namun, sayangnya, bahkan dengan pengelolaan gula darah yang tepat, ada kasus di mana retinopati terus berkembang.
  • Fotokoagulasi laser: Laser diterapkan pada retina untuk membakar area yang membutuhkan oksigen tinggi, menghambat perkembangan pembuluh darah baru. Ini adalah pengobatan yang efektif untuk mempertahankan penglihatan, tetapi tidak secara mendasar memperbaiki kerusakan retina.
  • Injeksi intravitreal obat anti-VEGF: Untuk menekan edema (pembengkakan) yang terjadi di retina dan pembuluh darah abnormal, obat yang menghambat faktor pertumbuhan endotel vaskular (VEGF) secara berkala disuntikkan ke dalam bola mata. Ini menekan perkembangan lesi pada banyak pasien, tetapi memerlukan injeksi sekitar sekali sebulan, yang merupakan beban ekonomi dan fisik...
  • Operasi vitreous: Ketika terjadi pendarahan vitreous atau ablasi retina yang disertai dengan pembuluh darah yang tumbuh, operasi vitreous menghilangkan pendarahan dalam mata dan jaringan parut. Ini adalah operasi yang memerlukan peralatan medis tingkat lanjut dan keterampilan, tetapi menjadi upaya terakhir untuk menghentikan lesi yang berkembang.

However, all current therapeutic methods are aimed at slowing or arresting the progression of diabetic retinopathy, rather than providing a definitive cure for the condition itself. Indeed, even with ongoing treatment, it remains challenging to entirely prevent future retinal damage and vision loss.

For instance, reports indicate that even after continuing anti-VEGF treatment for 2 years, only approximately 30% of patients experienced significant visual improvement, highlighting the limitations of treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the burden of routine visits and injections makes it challenging for many patients to adhere to treatment.

Consequently, while current treatments are capable of suppressing the progression of retinopathy, restoring damaged retinas to their original condition remains challenging, thereby elevating expectations for novel therapeutic modalities.

Chronic hyperglycemia damages blood vessels throughout the body. This can be likened to a road that has become cracked and difficult to traverse due to years of weathering. Healthy arteries resemble wide, clear pathways where blood flows unimpeded. However, when atherosclerosis develops, plaque (deposits of substances like cholesterol) accumulates within the vessels, narrowing these pathways. This impedes blood flow, preventing vital oxygen and nutrients from reaching their target tissues.

In diabetic retinopathy, this phenomenon affects the retinal microvasculature at the posterior pole of the eye. As these vessels become occluded and blood flow is compromised, retinal cells become hypoxic and damaged. Current treatment primarily involves symptomatic therapy aimed at suppressing neovascularization or hemorrhage resulting from this damage. In essence, the focus is on managing the immediate complications.

The ultimate goal, however, is to repair damaged blood vessels and the retinal tissue itself. To achieve this fundamental objective, stem cell therapy has garnered significant attention in recent years. Stem cells possess the ability to aid in the repair of damaged tissue and promote blood vessel regeneration, and are expected to reorganize the retinal microenvironment.

New Possibilities in Stem Cell Therapy and Regenerative Medicine for Diabetic Retinopathy

With the advancements in regenerative medicine in recent years, novel therapeutic approaches utilizing stem cells are being explored for diabetic retinopathy. Stem cells are specialized cells characterized by their capacity to differentiate into various cell types and their ability to facilitate tissue repair, essentially serving as the body's "reserves."

By administering stem cells to the patient's body, it becomes possible to regenerate and repair damaged retinal tissue, with the aim of restoring vision and healing lesions that were previously challenging with conventional treatments.

Indeed, research into stem cell therapy for diabetic retinopathy is ongoing worldwide. In animal experiments, it has been demonstrated that stem cells slow the progression of retinopathy and alleviate symptoms. For instance, studies have involved transplanting stem cells into the retinas of blind mice to restore the function of visual neuronal cells, as well as administering stem cell-derived factors to animals in a hyperglycemic state to suppress retinal inflammation.

Given the success of this pre-clinical research, stem cell therapy is showing great promise as a novel therapeutic option for diabetic retinopathy.

There are several types of stem cells. Stem cells derived from the patient's bone marrow and adipose tissue, as well as artificially created induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells), are currently under investigation. Among these, particular attention is being paid to umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (Wharton's Jelly-derived MSCs: WJ-MSCs), which are extracted from the Wharton's jelly portion of the umbilical cord.

WJ-MSCs, derived from fetal tissue, are characterized by their youthful properties and high proliferative capacity. Furthermore, the umbilical cord, typically discarded after birth, can be safely collected without burdening the infant or the pregnant mother, and without ethical concerns. Moreover, WJ-MSCs are known to exhibit low immunogenicity (high immune tolerance), allowing for the use of allogeneic cells derived from another individual's umbilical cord. Consequently, WJ-MSCs are considered a strong candidate for stem cell therapy in diabetic retinopathy.

Leveraging the potential of stem cells and regenerative medicine, it becomes possible to directly repair damaged retinal tissue and fundamentally treat the vision loss caused by diabetic retinopathy. This represents a distinct advantage of regenerative medicine, not present in current symptomatic therapies, and holds significant promise for patients.

Mechanism of Action of Stem Cell Therapy for Diabetic Retinopathy

Suppressing Inflammation and Aberrant Angiogenesis (Immune-Dampening)

In diabetic retinopathy, chronic inflammatory reactions occur in the retina due to hyperglycemic stress, leading to widespread damage. Stem cells release various substances that mitigate inflammation, effectively modulating the dysregulated immune response, stabilizing the retinal microenvironment, and suppressing the progression of pathological changes.

Furthermore, inflammatory neovascularization is also inhibited by stem cell-secreted factors. In this manner, stem cells ameliorate the retinal microenvironment and inhibit the progression of pathological alterations.

Vascular Repair and Promotion of Normal Blood Flow

Stem cells possess the ability to repair damaged tissues. In diabetic retinopathy, damaged capillaries and vascular networks pose a significant problem. However, stem cells can differentiate into cells that facilitate vascular repair (e.g., pericytes, which provide support around blood vessels) and have been reported to achieve this.

Indeed, animal experiments have demonstrated that administering stem cells into the eye increases retinal capillary density and improves blood flow. This ensures sufficient oxygen and nutrients reach the retina, thereby allowing for the amelioration of tissue damage and ischemic conditions.

Protecting and Regenerating Retinal Nerve Cells

The retina is part of the nervous tissue and contains crucial neural cells such as photoreceptors and retinal ganglion cells. Stem cells secrete numerous neurotrophic factors (such as neurotrophins) that provide neuroprotection and enhance the survival of injured retinal neurons.

For example, in a model of damaged mouse retinal nerves, when human WJ-MSCs were transplanted into the vitreous, the action of anti-inflammatory and neurotrophic factors secreted by the stem cells resulted in a significant reduction in retinal ganglion cell death...

In this manner, stem cells protect retinal nerve cells, and by promoting differentiation into new cells as required, are believed to lead to retinal tissue regeneration. Notably, the collection of substances secreted by stem cells is also termed the "secretome," and encompasses various factors, exemplified by exosomes (small vesicles with a diameter of approximately 50-150nm released by cells).

This secretome is the primary mediator of the therapeutic effects, and it can be said that stem cells heal the retina not merely by differentiating, but also by releasing beneficial factors. For instance, in a study where stem cell-derived exosomes were administered to diabetic model retinas, a specific signaling pathway (the Wnt/β-catenin pathway) that was dysregulated in the retina was suppressed, consequently reducing oxidative stress, inflammation, and the progression of neovascularization. These results suggest the possibility that stem cell-derived factors alone can effectively mitigate retinopathy.

Summary of Mechanism of Action

The therapeutic mechanism of stem cell therapy impacting the retina is not limited to a single pathway; rather, it is understood that the combined action of several mechanisms contributes to the therapeutic outcome. The principal modes of action are as follows:

  • Suppression of inflammation and aberrant vascular proliferation (immunosuppressive)
  • Repairing blood vessels and promoting normal blood flow.
  • Protecting and regenerating retinal nerve cells

Through such interventions, stem cell therapy holds the potential not only to halt the progression of diabetic retinopathy but also to restore the structure and function of the damaged retina.

Furthermore, in another study, bone marrow-derived CD34+ stem cells (possessing the ability to regenerate blood and blood vessels) administered to diabetic retinas resulted in the maintenance of retinal vascular tissue. In other words, through the use of stem cells, retinal vasculature is preserved, and blood flow deficiencies due to diabetes can be ameliorated.

Findings from Clinical Research (Human Verification)

The therapeutic effects of stem cell therapy are increasingly being reported in clinical studies involving human patients. For instance, in a clinical trial conducted in China, intravenous infusions of umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) were administered to 61 patients with type 2 diabetes, with an extended follow-up period of 3 years (PubMed ID: 27588104).

The results indicated that the group receiving stem cell injections demonstrated a lower incidence of complications, including diabetic retinopathy, compared to the control group that did not receive injections. Specifically, at the 3-year follow-up, the proportion of patients newly developing retinopathy was 16.1% in the stem cell therapy group versus 26.7% in the control group, a statistically significant difference. This suggests that stem cell therapy may contribute to the prevention and suppression of retinopathy progression.

Furthermore, in this trial, improvements in blood glucose control indicators (HbA1c and blood peptide levels) were also observed, suggesting the possibility that stem cell therapy improves whole-body glucose metabolism and indirectly alleviates retinopathy.

Nonetheless, these are significant findings demonstrating that stem cell therapy can have beneficial effects on the progression of diabetic retinopathy in humans, further increasing the prospects for larger clinical trials. It is important to note that no adverse side effects were observed during this treatment.

Currently, clinical trials for stem cell therapy for diabetic retinopathy are underway globally. It is anticipated that more definitive evidence will accumulate over the next few years. Consequently, the integration of stem cell therapy into clinical practice is rapidly approaching.

Safety and Side Effects of Stem Cell Therapy for Diabetic Retinopathy

When considering new treatment methods, safety is paramount. However, for stem cell therapy, a generally favorable safety profile has been reported in studies to date.

Specifically, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy is recognized for its superior safety profile compared to other cell therapies. MSCs carry a low risk of tumorigenicity (cancer formation) and are also known for their low immunogenicity, minimizing the risk of immune rejection. This allows for the potential safe administration of allogeneic cells.

In fact, in the aforementioned clinical study, no serious adverse events were observed in patients receiving stem cell infusions, and their safety was confirmed to be comparable to that of the control group receiving saline.

Thus, within the currently reported scope, stem cell therapy can impose a substantial burden on patients while being regarded as a highly safe treatment. Nevertheless, stem cell therapy for diabetic retinopathy remains in the investigational stage, and efforts are underway to safely achieve maximum efficacy. This method does not utilize the cells themselves, thereby avoiding the risks of immune responses or the proliferation of membrane-forming cells, and is anticipated to be clinically safer. Indeed, in animal studies, when stem cells were directly transplanted into the eye, transient inflammatory reactions were occasionally observed. However, when only the stem cell culture supernatant (conditioned medium) was injected, retinal inflammation did not occur; instead, neuronal cells were protected and visual function was enhanced. These findings have garnered attention as a novel method for achieving the "safety" and "efficacy" of stem cell therapy.

Overall, it can be stated that stem cell therapy presents fewer side effects compared to conventional treatment methods and can likely be confidently embraced. However, given its status as a novel treatment, it is crucial to obtain thorough explanations and ensure full comprehension before proceeding with the therapy. With further research, more safety data will be accumulated, and stem cell therapy for diabetic retinopathy is anticipated to become a safer and well-established medical treatment in the future.
