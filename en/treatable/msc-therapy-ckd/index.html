Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a condition characterized by a gradual decline in kidney function over a period of 3 months or more. As the disease progresses, the condition can become severe and may advance to uremia and end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

This disease is globally prevalent and is projected to continue to increase in the future. It is a serious condition that requires specialized management.

The principal causes of CKD include diabetes, hypertension, and chronic glomerulonephritis, leading to progressive deterioration of renal structure and function. As the disease progresses, the kidneys become unable to adequately excrete metabolic waste products, resulting in symptoms such as edema, fatigue, anorexia, and reduced quality of life.

For cases of CKD with significant renal function decline, renal replacement therapy such as dialysis or kidney transplantation becomes essential for sustaining life.

Current management of CKD primarily focuses on controlling the underlying disease and delaying progression, as well as providing symptomatic therapy for complications.

For instance, blood pressure and blood glucose management, dietary therapy, and pharmacotherapy (e.g., diuretics, RAS inhibitors, and more recently, SGLT2 inhibitors) are utilized to slow the deterioration of renal function. Recently, it has been demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibitors are effective in inhibiting the progression of CKD, and these agents are recommended in treatment guidelines.

Nevertheless, even with continued treatment, it remains challenging to fully arrest the progression of renal dysfunction, and many patients experience a gradual decline in kidney function despite ongoing therapy. Moreover, for patients with CKD stages 3 to 5 (moderate to end-stage), combinations of several drugs frequently prove insufficiently effective or are associated with significant adverse effects.

As kidney function progressively declines, patients will eventually require dialysis several times a week or a kidney transplant. Dialysis imposes a significant time and physical burden on patients, while kidney transplantation carries risks of organ rejection and donor shortages.

Thus, existing therapeutic approaches possess numerous limitations, and a pressing need exists for novel therapeutic modalities capable of "repairing the kidney itself and restoring its function."

Chronic Kidney Disease and Stem Cell Therapy: Novel Opportunities in Regenerative Medicine

In addressing this challenge, a recently emerging approach is regenerative medicine employing stem cell therapy. Stem cell therapy is a therapeutic method that utilizes "stem cells" (the foundational cells of our bodies) to promote the regeneration of damaged tissues.

In cases of renal impairment resulting from damage, the objective is to administer stem cells to promote the structural and functional regeneration of the compromised renal tissue.

Currently, stem cell therapy for chronic kidney disease is a subject of extensive research, with clinical studies and trials underway globally. The rationale for this research is the recently elucidated capacity of stem cells to halt the decline in renal function and even enhance it.

In fact, numerous basic studies and clinical trials have demonstrated that treatment utilizing stem cells (particularly mesenchymal stem cells) can enhance renal function through immune modulation and suppression of inflammation. Thus, stem cell therapy represents a novel therapeutic tool holding significant promise for CKD.

Notably, significant recent attention has been focused on Wharton's Jelly-derived umbilical cord stem cells (WJ-MSC) among mesenchymal stem cells (MSC).

Wharton's Jelly is a gelatinous tissue found within the umbilical cord, and the rich stem cells derived from this tissue offer numerous advantages as a cell source for regenerative medicine. Firstly, because they are extracted from umbilical cords obtained at birth, there is no burden on the donor, and ethical concerns are minimal as this tissue is typically discarded. Secondly, given their neonatal origin, the cells themselves are young and possess high proliferative capacity, facilitating the acquisition of a large number of stem cells through culture.

Furthermore, it has been reported that fetal-derived MSCs possess high immunomodulatory capabilities and a low risk of rejection, thereby facilitating allogeneic transplantation (the transfer of cells from a donor to a patient). Given the easy availability of donors, Wharton's Jelly-derived MSCs (WJ-MSCs) are anticipated to be a readily accessible stem cell source for numerous patients. Owing to these characteristics, WJ-MSCs have garnered particular attention in regenerative stem cell therapy for chronic kidney disease, and their application is progressing at the forefront of research.

Mechanism of Action of Stem Cell Therapy for Chronic Kidney Disease

Stem cell therapy is considered effective for CKD because stem cells are multifunctional therapeutic agents that perform multiple roles simultaneously. Two primary mechanisms are known to explain how stem cell therapy works in CKD.

Modulation of immune balance and suppression of inflammation

In Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), chronic inflammation occurs in the kidneys, or renal tissue is damaged by autoimmune reactions. Stem cells (particularly mesenchymal stem cells, MSCs) act to modulate such excessive immune reactions.

Specifically, stem cells secrete immunomodulatory substances within the body, which reduces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and concurrently activates the function of regulatory T cells, thereby contributing to the resolution of inflammation.

Stem cells modulate the immune system, akin to a command instructing an overactive immune response to attenuate. Through this action, the vicious cycle of autoimmune attack on the kidneys and inflammation is interrupted, and further renal tissue damage can be prevented.

Support for tissue repair and regeneration

Stem cells do not directly differentiate into renal cells, but rather, through various active substances they secrete (collectively known as the secretome), they promote the repair of the surrounding renal tissue.

This secretome contains growth factors and cytokines that support cell proliferation and repair, along with angiogenic factors, delivering survival and functional recovery signals to renal cells.

Additionally, stem cells suppress excessive fibrosis within the kidneys (a phenomenon characterized by the stiffening of damaged tissue into scar-like structures), promoting the formation of new capillaries that aid in the supply of oxygen and nutrients to the kidneys. In this manner, the internal renal environment is regulated, and tissues are reorganized to facilitate recovery.

Furthermore, the antioxidant substances secreted by stem cells protect renal cells by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (harmful substances that damage cells) generated within the kidneys, and are also reported to play a role in preventing renal cell death (apoptosis).

Thus, stem cell therapy functions as an intrinsic repair mechanism within the kidneys. It modulates a dysregulated immune system, revitalizes damaged cells, regulates blood flow and the local microenvironment, and creates conditions conducive to regeneration.

As a result, it not only halted the progression but also enabled the maximization of residual renal function while promoting renal recovery, subsequently garnering attention as a therapeutic approach.

Evidence of the Effectiveness of Stem Cell Therapy for Chronic Kidney Disease

Stem cell therapy holds theoretical promise, but the critical question is: Is it genuinely effective?

Although stem cell therapy is still a nascent medical field, a significant amount of research and clinical trials have been conducted to date, and evidence demonstrating its efficacy continues to accumulate.

Firstly, in preclinical research utilizing animal models, the demonstrable effects of stem cell therapy have been repeatedly confirmed. For instance, when MSCs are administered to animal models of renal failure (e.g., diabetic nephropathy or hypertensive renal damage), improvements in renal function and repair of renal tissue damage have been reported.

Indeed, research indicates that Wharton's Jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) exhibit remarkable capabilities in restoring renal function and repairing damaged renal tissue in animal models of chronic renal failure. These findings are instrumental in establishing the concept of regenerative medicine for renal treatment, moving it beyond the animal experimentation phase.

Furthermore, early-phase clinical trials (Phase I and II) targeting a small number of patients are also being conducted. Overall results are positive, and it has been reported that CKD patients receiving stem cell therapy showed stabilization or improvement in indicators of renal function (GFR = glomerular filtration rate) and a reduction in proteinuria (protein in urine).

In other words, indicators that were expected to deteriorate with the progression to renal failure instead demonstrated improvement or maintenance attributable to stem cell therapy.

For instance, it has been reported that in patients with severe diabetic nephropathy (CKD stage 5), WJ-MSCs were administered via infusion. Several months post-treatment, renal function test values significantly improved, and the patients' quality of life also increased. In these cases, eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) values improved, enabling a delay in the initiation of dialysis. Furthermore, in a separate clinical trial, when MSCs were administered to patients with lupus nephritis, a difficult-to-treat autoimmune disease, a significant reduction in urinary proteinuria was reported post-treatment and during long-term follow-up.

While these findings are preliminary, the overall research reported to date strongly indicates the potential efficacy of stem cell therapy for CKD.

A further important point is that effects are observed within a favorable safety context. As detailed below, the treatment was administered without significant adverse effects, and the stabilization or improvement of kidney disease was reported in several studies, presenting a very promising outlook when considering the viability of this therapeutic approach.

While ongoing clinical trials certainly necessitate increasing case numbers and continuously accumulating scientific evidence, the gradually accumulating scientific knowledge, both domestically and internationally, indicates definite progress. Stem cell therapy, as a treatment method capable of challenging the long-held belief that 'kidney disease is incurable,' is beginning to demonstrate clear prospects.

Safety of Stem Cell Therapy for Chronic Kidney Disease and Side Effects

Patient safety is a paramount concern when new treatments are introduced. With regard to stem cell therapy, patients naturally raise questions such as: "Is it genuinely safe?" and "Are there any adverse effects?"

Regarding this point, the data obtained from research to date indicates very reassuring results. Firstly, serious adverse events from MSC administration have not been reported in numerous clinical trials.

CKD patients undergoing stem cell therapy exhibited a post-treatment course free of apparent adverse effects, and the procedure was reported as safely implementable. Indeed, specific clinical trials have also concluded that "MSC infusion can be safely administered, with no observed adverse events."

Thus, at this point, available information indicates that stem cell therapy demonstrates an excellent safety profile.

Why is the safety profile considered high? One of the reasons is that MSCs themselves possess characteristics that result in low immunogenicity.

MSCs are not cells that actively stimulate the immune system; rather, they are cells that suppress inflammation. Consequently, even when cells from another donor are administered, serious rejection reactions or immune complications are unlikely. In other words, they are "well-tolerated cells."

Additionally, the cells utilized for therapeutic applications are cultured in specialized cell culture facilities under stringent quality control. Following comprehensive safety testing, including sterility assays and confirmation of genetic/chromosomal stability, they are subsequently administered to patients.

With such a management system, the risk of infection and abnormal cell proliferation is minimized to the greatest extent possible. Furthermore, the stem cell administration method utilizes minimally invasive approaches, such as intravenous infusion, thereby avoiding invasive procedures like major surgery.

In light of the above, stem cell therapy is considered a safe and viable treatment based on current understanding.

While temporary mild symptoms such as low-grade fever or pain at the injection site may occur as adverse events, no more serious effects have been reported. Researchers and clinicians maintained close surveillance; however, overall, this treatment is deemed to carry minimal risk for patients.

This high safety profile also contributes to the optimism surrounding stem cell therapy.

Conclusion

Stem cell therapy for chronic kidney disease is a field within regenerative medicine research that currently holds the highest promise. The previously elusive concept of "restoring kidney vitality" is now beginning to gain a tangible foothold in reality.

Certainly, significant further research and time are still required before it can be established as a standard treatment. However, the steadily accumulating knowledge, both domestically and internationally, demonstrates tangible progress. In the future, stem cell therapy may complement or even replace conventional treatments (pharmacotherapy, dialysis, and transplantation), potentially enabling CKD patients to avoid dependence on dialysis or transplantation.

For patients and families, this is a topic that brings great hope and confidence.

Although stem cell therapy is a nascent medical technology, its potential is supported by research, and positive reports are beginning to emerge from patients who have undergone treatment. Crucially, the most important message is that kidney disease does not have to be incurable.

For those grappling with chronic kidney disease, this research offers a beacon of hope. It is anticipated that this treatment method will continue to evolve and ultimately be safely made available to a wider patient population.
