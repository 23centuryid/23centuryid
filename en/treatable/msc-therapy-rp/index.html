Stem Cell Therapy for Retinitis Pigmentosa

Genesis: The State of RP and the Blessing of Hope

Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP) is a rare genetic disease characterized by the progressive degeneration of retinal photoreceptor cells due to genetic abnormalities. This leads to a narrowing of the visual field and ultimately results in severe, progressive, and incurable vision impairment.

To date, there is no definitive cure for RP. Treatments are limited to symptomatic interventions, such as vitamin A supplementation and cataract surgery, which only marginally slow the disease's progression. Consequently, many patients and their families live with the anxiety that "the decline in vision cannot be halted."

However, in recent years, reports have begun to emerge indicating that certain treatments can indeed enhance visual function. A beacon of hope has started to illuminate the medical field. For instance, in gene therapy treatments for specific types of RP gene mutations, cases where improvements in visual field and vision were observed have begun to surface.

However, RP is a highly heterogeneous disease encompassing more than 260 distinct genetic types. Consequently, a gene-specific therapeutic drug development approach presents inherent limitations. Against this backdrop, the prospect of stem cell therapy—which aims to "preserve the retina itself, irrespective of the genetic mutation type"—is rapidly gaining traction.

Stem cell therapy offers the potential to repair and regenerate the retina, thereby halting disease progression. This capability is already beginning to yield promising early results in some RP patients. The historical necessity of 'resigning oneself to vision loss' is now transitioning into an era where vision preservation may be achievable. Stem cell therapy is currently paving the way for this transformation.

Potential Vision Improvement from Stem Cell Therapy—Three Functions Supporting "Retina-Protective Power"

Promoting Regeneration of the Injured Retina

Stem cells possess the capacity to differentiate into cells with properties similar to photoreceptor cells or retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells under specific conditions. Leveraging this capability, it is anticipated that depleted photoreceptor cells can be replaced, thereby restoring light responsiveness.

For instance, it has been reported that when stem cells were transplanted into Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP) patients with significant vision loss, both retinal thickness and electrical responses recovered. In other words, even if complete vision recovery is not achievable, there is hope that "some visual function may be restored."

Protecting Surviving Visual Cells

Stem cells secrete neurotrophic and growth factors within the retina. This supports the function of residual visual cells, thereby facilitating the preservation of existing vision.

This effect is also known as the "paracrine effect," wherein stem cells positively influence their surroundings. Even without differentiating into new cells, it confers substantial benefits upon the retina.

Indeed, there have been reports that some patients experienced a "widening of the visual field after treatment" or "becoming slightly more capable in darkness."

Inhibiting Inflammation and Immune Response that Promotes Progression

It has become evident that the pathogenesis of Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP) involves not only genetic defects in photoreceptor cells but also chronic inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation. Stem cells also possess the capacity to mitigate this inflammation and immune-mediated damage.

Anti-inflammatory cytokines secreted by stem cells are believed to modulate the retinal environment and mitigate damage to visual cells. This essentially functions by downregulating dysfunctional immune responses and stabilizing the retinal milieu, thereby enabling visual cells to prioritize regeneration.

These three forces serve as the foundation for addressing the development of RP.

The appeal of stem cell therapy is that it is not limited to a single function, but possesses the capacity to support the retina through various mechanisms.

  • For depleted cells: The capacity for new cell generation (regeneration).
  • For fatigued cells: The power to maintain, support, and protect.
  • For excessive inflammation: The power to soothe and rebalance (regulation)

Through various synergistic interactions, the entire retinal environment achieves optimal organization, thereby establishing a foundation for the preservation of vision.

KEY POINTS:

  • Stem cells possess the capacity to differentiate into visual cells or RPE cells to facilitate regeneration.
  • It is expected to support the maintenance of vision through the secretion of nutritional factors, thereby protecting existing visual cells.
  • It is possible to slow the rate of progression by suppressing inflammation and abnormal immune responses.
  • The intrinsic value of stem cell therapy is derived from three key mechanisms: regeneration, protection, and inflammation suppression.

Latest Clinical Trials in RP Patients and Results

Enhancement of Visual Acuity and Visual Field with a Single Administration of Umbilical Cord-Derived MSCs

A study was conducted in which Wharton's Jelly mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSC) were transplanted once into the posterior segment of the eye (sub-Tenon's) of patients with Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP). During 1-year post-treatment follow-up:

  • Visual acuity improved, with the number of legible characters increasing by more than 10 (an approximate 2-line improvement on a vision test).
  • Retinal thickness was restored, and the photoreceptive portion (visual cell layer) increased.
  • In a test evaluating retinal function (electroretinogram), responses also improved.

This increase was observed regardless of gene type (dominant or recessive). Therefore, for individuals concerned about the effect's applicability to their specific genetic makeup, these results provide reassurance.

Confirmation of Improved Visual Acuity and Safety with Autologous Bone Marrow Stem Cells

At the University of California, Davis, research involved the injection of autologous bone marrow-derived stem cells, collected from the patients' own bones, into their eyes (7 participants). The findings:

  • Objective vision improvement was confirmed in 4 out of 7 individuals.
  • The field of view is also maintained or partially enhanced.
  • Absence of adverse effects, excellent safety profile.

The congruence between patient-reported experiences of "improved vision" and objective test values is of significant importance. This marks a crucial point where "change" becomes evident within the relentless progression of RP.

Emphasis on Development Confirmed in 3-Year Monitoring

In another study, RP patients who received periodic administration of WJ-MSCs combined with visual rehabilitation (magnetic stimulation training) were monitored for 3 years. Compared to the untreated group:

  • The rate of field of view shrinkage is significantly slower.
  • Retinal function tests (electroretinogram) also indicate a maintenance trend.
  • Vision is generally stable, with many cases showing no progression.

Merely by halting or slowing the progression, the sense of security in the daily lives of RP patients is significantly enhanced. This trial yields valuable insights, demonstrating that stem cell therapy can alter the course of the disease.

The fact of an increase in cases is our greatest hope.

Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP) is a disease where significant improvement is rarely observed, often leading to the sentiment, "no matter what I do, it's useless." However, the finding from this clinical study, demonstrating that some individuals experienced an improvement in visual function—even if only in a few instances—has become a pivotal point in reinforcing the belief that "further action is still possible."

KEY POINTS:

  • A single administration of WJ-MSC demonstrated improvements in vision, visual field, and retinal response.
  • Objective visual improvement was confirmed in 4 of 7 individuals treated with autologous bone marrow stem cells, demonstrating a favorable safety profile.
  • In the 3-year monitoring study, clear developmental inhibition was demonstrated.
  • The existence of documented cases where visual function has improved is the strongest hope for RP patients.
