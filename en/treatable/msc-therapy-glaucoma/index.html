Glaucoma is a progressive disease in which the optic nerve at the back of the eye gradually deteriorates, and the visual field progressively narrows. The disease often develops without obvious symptoms, and patients are unaware until a portion of their visual field is lost. In severe cases, it can lead to blindness.

A contributing factor is elevated intraocular pressure (IOP); however, glaucoma can also manifest with normal intraocular pressure (normal-tension glaucoma). Retinal ganglion cells, which constitute the optic nerve, are components of the central nervous system. Once damaged and lost, these cells are unable to naturally regenerate. Consequently, at present, it is deemed impossible to restore lost visual fields.

In fact, glaucoma is one of the leading causes of blindness worldwide, affecting an estimated 76 million people in 2020, and projected to affect over 111 million people by 2040.

Current Status of Glaucoma Management and Challenges to be Addressed

Intraocular pressure management is currently the primary treatment.

Glaucoma is a disease in which intraocular pressure increases, leading to progressive damage to the optic nerve. Therefore, current standard treatment focuses on lowering eye pressure to alleviate the burden on the optic nerve. Primary treatments include:

  • Ophthalmic drops: Pharmacology for lowering intraocular pressure (e.g., prostaglandin analogs, beta-blockers, etc.)
  • Laser treatment: Laser trabeculoplasty to enhance aqueous humor outflow (intraocular fluid).
  • Procedure: Trabeculotomy and shunt tube implantation to reduce intraocular pressure

This treatment can slow the progression of visual impairment, but at present, there is no method to repair damaged optic nerves.

The limitation of focusing solely on development:

Indeed, there are numerous cases where visual fields continue to gradually deteriorate even with ongoing treatment. For patients, this leaves a significant sense of frustration: "All that is achieved is merely preventing further progression, and lost visual fields will not be recovered."

In current medical practice, complete reversal of optic nerve damage caused by glaucoma is not possible; treatment remains symptomatic, aimed at delaying progression.

Consequently, there is a pressing need for novel therapeutic approaches capable of regenerating and repairing the optic nerve itself.

Towards Optic Nerve Regeneration: Challenges of Stem Cell Therapy in Glaucoma

A regenerative approach that transcends conventional boundaries:

Conventional glaucoma treatment historically focused on lowering intraocular pressure, with no means of restoring damaged optic nerves. To address this challenge, increasing attention is being directed towards stem cell therapy, a branch of regenerative medicine.

Stem cell therapy is a treatment method in which young, healthy cells, either autologous (from the patient) or allogeneic (from a donor), are introduced into the body to promote the repair of damaged tissues or the restoration of function. In glaucoma, there is also hope that lost optic nerve cells can be compensated for, thereby guiding visual recovery.

MSC derived from umbilical cord of interest:

Among stem cells, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are particularly promising in regenerative medicine within ophthalmology. MSCs are found in bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and other sources. However, special attention is given to umbilical cord-derived MSCs, specifically Wharton's Jelly-derived MSCs (WJ-MSCs), many of which are contained within Wharton's Jelly, a tissue located within the umbilical cord.

  • Acquisition Safety: The umbilical cord is naturally obtained during delivery, thereby imposing no burden on the donor (newborn or mother).
  • Youthful and active cells: With minimal age-related decline, they maintain a high capacity for proliferation and differentiation even in long-term culture.
  • Immunological privilege: Allogeneic transplantation is also not readily subject to immune rejection.
  • High safety: Risk of tumorigenesis (malignant transformation) is extremely low.

Given these characteristics, WJ-MSCs are an ideal cell source suitable for regenerative medicine and clinical research, with ongoing clinical applications worldwide. Indeed, recent research reviews also report that treatments utilizing umbilical cord MSCs show promising results for optic nerve disorders, including glaucoma.

Mechanism of Action of Stem Cells on the Glaucomatous Optic Nerve

Protecting nerve cells (neuroprotective effect):

MSCs abundantly secrete neuroprotective proteins such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF). These factors support compromised retinal ganglion cells by providing essential nutrients and enhancing their survival rates. This mechanism is analogous to supplying ample water and fertilizer to a wilting plant to restore its vitality. The therapeutic cocktail released by MSCs facilitates the repair of neuronal damage and robustly supports these cells.

Reducing inflammation (immune modulation effect):

MSC attenuates excessive inflammatory responses and fosters a quiescent environment for the optic nerve. Specifically, it reduces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and conversely promotes the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10). Analogous to swiftly quelling a rampant blaze, MSC suppresses ocular inflammation and establishes a conducive environment for the undisturbed survival of neural cells.

Enhances blood flow and delivers oxygen and nutrients:

Growth factors secreted by MSCs (e.g., VEGF, HGF) promote neovascularization around the optic nerve and stimulate blood flow. Consequently, nerve cells receive sufficient oxygen and nutrients, and a healthy environment is established to withstand damage. Analogously, this is akin to introducing new irrigation channels to a drought-stricken field, enabling plants to flourish.

Possibility of tissue repair and regeneration:

MSCs possess the inherent ability to differentiate into specific cell types as required, thereby facilitating the repair of damaged tissue. In the context of glaucoma, regeneration is sought for two targets:

  • Trabecular repair: If MSCs differentiate into trabecular cells that aid in aqueous humor outflow, there is potential to fundamentally improve intraocular pressure control.
  • Retinal Ganglion Cell Regeneration: The potential exists for Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs) to augment retinal visual neurons and establish new neural networks. Experimental reports indicate that stem cell-derived neurons have successfully integrated into the retina, responded to light stimuli, and transmitted visual signals, significantly raising future expectations.

Expected Effects of Stem Cell Therapy for Glaucoma

Promising results demonstrated in animal models:

Previous research has demonstrated that stem cell therapy exhibits promising effects for glaucoma in animal models. Upon injection of bone marrow-derived MSCs into the eye, an increase in the survival rate of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) was observed, and protection of optic nerve fibers (axons) was confirmed. In a separate study, it was reported that MSCs localized near the trabecular meshwork and improved aqueous humor outflow by reducing intraocular pressure.

These results demonstrate the potential of MSCs to protect the optic nerve and bidirectionally control intraocular pressure.

Promising signs in human clinical trials:

Optimism is also growing regarding the safety and efficacy of stem cell therapy in human clinical trials. In initial clinical trials, a treatment involving the ocular transplantation of a stem cell-containing device was safely performed, and a trend of mild improvement in visual field tests was observed. Furthermore, the reduction in the thickness of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) was also suppressed, suggesting the potential to halt the progression of optic nerve atrophy.

Furthermore, in a meta-analysis published in 2024, stem cell treatment was administered to 66 eyes of patients with optic nerve diseases (including glaucoma), and the results demonstrated a statistically significant mean improvement in vision. These findings are considered to introduce new possibilities to the previously held notion that "lost visual fields are irreversible."

Expected Safety of Stem Cell Therapy for Glaucoma

Demonstrated security track record

Mesenchymal Stem Cell (MSC) therapy for glaucoma has demonstrated a favorable safety profile in current clinical studies. Umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) exhibit low immunogenicity, making them less prone to rejection even when administered to allogeneic recipients. Indeed, in clinical trials conducted within ophthalmology, no serious adverse events (e.g., infection, tumorigenesis) have been reported. UC-MSCs maintain cellular integrity, and the risk of genetic abnormalities or malignancy during proliferative culture has been confirmed to be exceedingly low. Thus, MSCs are evaluated as cells with an excellent safety profile, possessing inherent differentiation capacity without exhibiting uncontrolled plasticity.

Notes on Operational Security Management and Considerations

Stem cell therapy is a medical procedure that requires proper management and oversight. Intraocular cell injection necessitates aseptic management to prevent rare instances of infection and inflammation. However, these risks are mitigated by the same measures employed for existing treatments such as anti-VEGF injections. Following MSC administration, transient ocular redness and blurred vision may occasionally be observed, but the majority of these reactions are mild, transient, and resolve spontaneously.

Previous reports indicated no cases of significant visual deterioration attributable to stem cell therapy; conversely, many patients reported positive improvements post-surgery. To ensure safe treatment, it is essential that care is administered under a professional management system at medical institutions with advanced quality management for stem cell culture and enhanced aseptic techniques.

A Novel Perspective Offered by Regenerative Medicine

Stem cell therapy unlocks new possibilities in the previously unattainable area of optic nerve regeneration. An era is dawning where regenerative medicine offers renewed hope for visual function lost due to glaucoma. The potency and safety of stem cell-mediated repair expand treatment options, offering positive prospects for many patients.

Presently, researchers worldwide are diligently working towards clinical application, and significant progress is steadily being achieved. Glaucoma has long been considered an incurable disease, but with a steadfast commitment to research and development, a beacon of hope is beginning to emerge.

To patients and families with glaucoma who are concerned about vision loss, please be aware that advancements in stem cell therapy are ongoing, and the future of such therapies holds significant promise. For further information on stem cell therapy for glaucoma or to consult regarding treatment eligibility, please contact a specialized medical institution or your treating physician.
