The rationale for the very low rejection risk of Wharton's Jelly cells derived from the umbilical cord.

22 Maret 2025
  • Stem Cell Therapy & Regenerative Care

Wharton's Jelly cells are stem cells isolated from Wharton's Jelly, a substance found within the umbilical cord tissue collected at birth. Wharton's Jelly is a soft connective tissue that encases the blood vessels of the umbilical cord, and this region is rich in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs).

Specifically, Wharton's Jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) are reported to have a reduced risk of rejection upon allogeneic transplantation and are considered highly promising for next-generation stem cell therapies.

In this article, we elucidate the scientific basis for the low immunogenicity of WJ-MSC, by delving into the complex immunological background, thereby enabling you to undergo stem cell therapy in Malaysia with confidence.

What is Rejection? Its relationship with HLA.

In medical transplantation, rejection is a reaction in which the immune system recognizes the transplanted organ or cells as foreign and attempts to attack and eliminate them.

Our immune system is typically responsible for protecting the body from pathogens such as viruses and bacteria; however, in transplant therapy, this defensive function causes complications.

For instance, in organ transplantation, when immune cells recognize markers on the surface of a transplanted organ as foreign, lymphocytes and other cells initiate an attack, leading to damage of the transplanted organ. This phenomenon is known as "rejection."

Proteins known as Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLA) are critically involved in this immune identification process. HLA molecules are present on the surface of all cells, functioning as a unique cellular identifier for each individual. Immune cells examine these HLA markers to distinguish between "self" and "non-self."

Therefore, in transplantation, when the HLA types of the donor and recipient are mismatched, the immune system tends to perceive the transplanted cells as foreign, thereby increasing the risk of rejection.

To prevent such rejection, medical institutions typically conduct thorough compatibility testing to maximize the compatibility of donor and recipient HLA types. Furthermore, if a risk of rejection persists, immunosuppressive agents can be utilized as needed to suppress immune function and ensure a safe transplantation.

Why Wharton's Jelly Stem Cells Are So Compelling

WJ-MSCs possess several immunological characteristics that contribute to their low immunogenicity. Key features include:

Very Low HLA Antigen Expression

One characteristic of WJ-MSCs is the low expression of Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) on their cell surface. HLA serves as a crucial marker utilized by immune cells to differentiate between "self" and "non-self," and it is a key molecule in immune rejection during transplantation therapy.

More specifically, MHC class I antigens (HLA-ABC) are expressed at very low levels, and MHC class II antigens (HLA-DR), which readily react with allogeneic immune systems, are virtually unexpressed.

Owing to this low expression, WJ-MSCs are not readily recognized by immune cells, particularly T cells, as foreign entities.

Consequently, the immunogenic properties of WJ-MSCs—their capacity to elicit an immune response—are significantly suppressed, and high engraftment rates can be anticipated even for allogeneic transplantation (cells from another individual).

Lacks co-stimulatory signals for immune cell activation.

Typically, for the immune system to mount a strong response to foreign substances, two signals are required. The first signal is the recognition of "HLA antigens," and the second signal is an additional stimulus referred to as a "co-stimulatory signal."

Lymphocytes, such as T cells and B cells, require both signals for full activation and to commence their attack.

Co-stimulatory signals are mediated by molecules such as CD80 and CD86; however, research has demonstrated that Wharton's Jelly stem cells (WJ-MSCs) show negligible expression of these co-stimulatory molecules.

Therefore, even if WJ-MSCs are detected by immune cells, the second signal, which would typically transmit an "attack command," is not relayed. This prevents full T cell activation and consequently reduces the likelihood of rejection.

This feature is a primary factor enabling WJ-MSC to be well-tolerated within the body.

Secreting immunosuppressive cytokines to suppress inflammation

Wharton's Jelly stem cells (WJ-MSCs) are not only resistant to immune system attack, but also possess a regulatory function that modulates the activity of the surrounding immune system.

Its primary mechanism is cytokine secretion, which are intercellular signaling molecules.

WJ-MSCs are known to secrete high levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as Interleukin 10 (IL-10) and Transforming Growth Factor β (TGF-β), which have an immunosuppressive effect on excessive immune responses.

These substances inhibit excessive immune cell activity and mitigate inflammatory responses, creating an environment where immune responses are less likely to occur.

In this manner, WJ-MSC establishes a local environment where immune responses are attenuated. Consequently, cells are more stable within the body after transplantation and readily engraft. This represents a crucial element contributing to the prevention of rejection.

Expressing HLA-G, a molecule involved in immune tolerance

Wharton's Jelly stem cells (WJ-MSCs) are known to express a specific HLA molecule type called "HLA-G." HLA-G is an "immune tolerance factor" that functions to prevent fetal rejection by the maternal immune system during pregnancy, and is also naturally occurring.

Through the expression of HLA-G, when WJ-MSCs are transplanted into the body, the cells become less susceptible to immune cell attack. HLA-G is known to promote the increase of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which suppress immune function, and has been found to mitigate exaggerated immune responses.

Thus, WJ-MSC not only possesses an immunologically privileged structure, but also has the ability to actively modulate immune responses and create a tolerant environment within the body.

Wharton's Jelly does not contain blood.

One of the reasons why Wharton's Jelly mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) exhibit a lower probability of rejection is the fact that Wharton's Jelly tissue does not contain blood components.

This bears significant implications for immunological safety during allogeneic WJ-MSC transplantation.

Wharton's Jelly, located in the umbilical cord, is a connective tissue that encases blood vessels. Due to its location, this tissue does not contain red blood cells or white blood cells. Consequently, when stem cells harvested from this region are transplanted, the likelihood of donor blood group antigen admixture or donor immune cells (lymphocytes) is very low.

This means that the risk of donor blood component transfer can be avoided, which constitutes a significant technical and immunological advantage.

It has been confirmed that even when stem cells derived from other tissues, such as bone marrow, are transplanted, ABO blood group antigens are minimally expressed. Therefore, stem cell transplantation can be successful even without blood group compatibility.

However, given that WJ-MSC is free from blood-derived components from the collection stage, it serves as a reassuring additive.

Furthermore, because Wharton's Jelly-derived cell products are less likely to contain donor immune cells such as T cells and B cells, the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), where the graft attacks the recipient's immune system, is also significantly low.

Therefore, the avascular structure serves as a complementary factor in preventing immune rejection for WJ-MSC, and holds significant implications.

Comparison of Rejection Risk: Wharton's Jelly, Bone Marrow, and Adipose-Derived Stem Cells

In stem cell therapy, immune rejection reactions against transplanted cells are a critical issue to avoid. Here, we compare three types of stem cells: Wharton's Jelly-derived stem cells, bone marrow-derived stem cells, and adipose-derived stem cells (with the latter assuming autologous transplantation).

Wharton's Jelly Mesenchymal Stem Cell (WJ-MSC)

  • WJ-MSC expresses very low levels of HLA antigens, particularly exhibiting virtually no expression of HLA-DR, which is known to readily react with immune cells.
  • Furthermore, it lacks T cell co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80, CD86) and possesses a structure resistant to immune cell attack.
  • Secreting high levels of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby creating an environment where inflammation is suppressed and immune function remains quiescent.
  • These cells are derived from the umbilical cord of a newborn, are exceptionally young, and remain unaffected by environmental damage or aging.
  • The isolated Wharton's Jelly tissue does not contain blood components, thereby ensuring a very low risk of ABO blood group antigen or donor immune cell admixture, and a high safety profile.
  • Based on these characteristics, WJ-MSCs are anticipated to exhibit exceptionally high transplantation compatibility, even for allogeneic transplantation.

Bone Marrow-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cell (BM-MSC)

  • Bone marrow-derived MSCs express HLA class I (HLA-ABC) at a moderate level, and HLA-DR (class II) expression is readily inducible in an inflammatory bodily environment, rendering them susceptible to immune targeting.
  • In certain situations, co-stimulatory molecules can be expressed, which can activate T cells and trigger rejection responses.
  • Despite the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β, their inhibitory effects demonstrated lower suppressive potency compared to WJ-MSC, and varied depending on the individual's age.
  • Bone marrow collection is an invasive procedure, and for cells derived from older donors, concerns exist regarding increased immunogenicity and functional decline.
  • Given this background, BM-MSCs are relatively safe for autotransplantation; however, for allogeneic transplantation, immunosuppressive measures may be required.

Adipose-Derived Stem Cell (AD-MSC, assuming autologous transplantation)

  • In autotransplantation using stem cells derived from their own adipose tissue, the immune system does not recognize them as foreign; consequently, there is theoretically no risk of rejection.
  • Given the identical HLA type, the probability of immune rejection is extremely low, and prior compatibility testing is not required.
  • However, it cannot be entirely excluded that unexpected reactions may occur, contingent upon the local immune environment, when transplanted into an inflamed area.
  • Furthermore, cells obtained from donor adipose tissue are influenced by the donor's age and health status, and both cell quality and regenerative capacity can readily vary among individuals.
  • While autologous transplantation offers very high immunologic safety against rejection, challenges persist concerning cell function and effect stability.

Summary

The reduced likelihood of rejection for Wharton's Jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs) stems from several unique immunological characteristics. Firstly, their low expression of Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLAs) makes them difficult for immune cells to detect, granting them an "immunologically inconspicuous" nature. Secondly, they almost entirely lack co-stimulatory molecules that activate T-cells, thereby maintaining an "immuno-inert" state. Furthermore, by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, and by expressing HLA-G, which is involved in immune tolerance, WJ-MSCs possess the ability to actively promote immune acceptance. The combination of these elements allows allogeneic WJ-MSCs to be readily accepted by the host and easily engraft following transplantation. The intrinsic "immune tolerance" characteristics of WJ-MSCs make them highly unique cells from an immunological perspective and a highly promising option in stem cell and regenerative therapies. Research is currently ongoing, and in the future, WJ-MSCs are expected to serve as a "universal stem cell source" that can be utilized without concerns about rejection, leading to broader applications in various medical treatments.
